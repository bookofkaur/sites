Building a College List That Actually Fits You

By Darrian Belcher7 min readApplications · College List

Creating an organized college list is one of the first steps in the application process — and one of the most important. A great list is a diverse group of colleges that actually match your needs, preferences, and financial resources. Here's the 5-step process I used to build mine.

Step 1: Ask Yourself What You Actually Want

Before you Google a single school, get clear on what matters to you — not what your parents want or where your friends are going.

  • Location: Do you want to leave home? Big city or small town? Where do you want to spend the next 2–4 years?
  • Major: What do you want to study? What subjects do you actually enjoy?
  • Size: Large campus with big lecture classes, or something smaller and closer-knit?
  • Sports: Do you want to compete in college — and at what level (D1, D2, D3)?
  • Extracurriculars: What activities do you want to keep doing? Are any a must-have?

Keep your answers in mind as you search — they're the filter for everything that follows.

Step 2: Build a Large Initial List (10–30 Schools)

Cast a wide net. Two resources that helped me the most:

  • College Board's BigFuture — a database of thousands of colleges searchable by major, size, acceptance rate, location, and more.
  • Niche — another strong search tool for matching schools to your preferences.

Step 3: Research and Narrow Down (8–16 Schools)

Dig into each school on your initial list and cut it down to schools you'd genuinely be excited to attend. Ways to learn more:

  • Review the college's website
  • Email admissions counselors
  • Attend online info sessions or college fairs (virtual or in-person)
  • Ask parents, teachers, or alumni who've been there
  • Take a virtual tour or watch student-made YouTube videos
  • Visit in person, if you can

Check the Net Price, Not the Sticker Price

A college's net price is the total cost for one year minus grants and scholarships (money you don't pay back). It varies school to school based on their financial aid policies and your family's finances. Every college has a net price calculator — search "[college name] net price calculator" or use the College Board's tool. It'll ask about your family's income, state of residency, GPA, and test scores, then estimate what you'd actually pay.

💡 Don't cross a school off just because its net price looks high. That number is an estimate, and some colleges with a scary sticker price have the best financial aid. Use it to compare, not to eliminate.

Step 4: Organize Into Safety, Likely, and Reach

Once you've narrowed your list, sort each school by comparing your GPA and test scores to their averages. Here's how I categorized mine — I had a 4.36 GPA and a 1320 SAT when I started applying:

  • Safety schools — you're almost certain to get in. My example: Virginia Commonwealth University (avg GPA 3.65, avg SAT 1150, 86% acceptance rate). My stats were well above their average.
  • Likely schools — a good chance, your stats are close to their average. My example: Florida State University (avg GPA 4.1, avg SAT 1270, 37% acceptance rate).
  • Reach schools — could be tough, their average stats are higher than yours. My example: University of North Carolina (avg GPA 4.4, avg SAT 1400, 23% acceptance rate).

Build a balanced list across all three categories — don't add a safety or reach just to check a box, and don't skip reach schools out of fear. And remember: stats aren't everything. Essays and extracurriculars carry real weight in admissions, even when your numbers fall a little short of a school's average.

Step 5: Reflect and Revise

Congratulate yourself — you did it. Your list will keep evolving as you dig deeper into the process, but having a foundation gives you a clear place to start. Next up: registering for (or retaking) the SAT or ACT if you haven't locked in a score yet.
