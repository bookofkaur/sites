How I Got Into 25 Colleges — And Why I Built College Confused

By Darrian Belcher7 min readApplications · My Story

My older sister went through the college application process a few years before I did, and she helped me a ton — walking me through the Common App, reading my essays, pointing me toward scholarships and FAFSA, and talking me off the ledge more than once when it got overwhelming. I got lucky. Not every student has an older sibling who's already been through it and is willing to walk them through it step by step. This site exists because I wanted every student to have the version of that help I had — even if it's not an actual person, just a place that explains things clearly and doesn't assume you already know how any of this works.

The Gap Is Bigger Than It Should Be

This isn't just a personal story — it's a structural problem. The American School Counselor Association recommends one counselor for every 250 students. The actual national average is about 372 students per counselor, and in some states it's far worse. One in five students in the U.S. attends a school with zero access to a counselor at all — roughly 8 million students nationwide. If you don't have someone like my sister — a family member, an older friend, anyone who's done this before and has the time to walk you through it — there's often no one whose job it is to walk you through any of this either.

That gap hits hardest exactly where it matters most: research shows first-gen students are significantly less familiar with terms like FAFSA and the Master Promissory Note, less likely to know deadlines, and more likely to miss aid opportunities simply because no one in their household has navigated this bureaucracy before. None of that is about ability. It's about access to information other families take for granted.

What Actually Worked for Me

I got into 25+ schools and received 10+ full-ride offers — including Virginia State University, Virginia Commonwealth University, Alabama A&M, Fisk University, Xavier University of Louisiana, Bethune-Cookman, and William & Mary. Along the way I won $1M+ in scholarships, including the Thurgood Marshall College Fund's VISA Black Scholars award (about $20,000 a year — my biggest single win) and the Trajectory Foundation Scholarship. My sister helped me stay organized and pointed me toward opportunities I wouldn't have found on my own — but the volume of work was still mine. Nobody fills out 40 scholarship applications for you. It happened because I treated the process like something learnable, and I didn't stop after the first setback.

What I'd tell myself at the start

  • Assume nothing is obvious — and that's fine. Terms like "Early Decision," "net price," and "Student Aid Index" aren't intuitive to anyone hearing them for the first time. Look everything up. There's no such thing as a dumb question about a process you were never taught.
  • Your counselor is a resource, even if they're stretched thin. With a caseload of 300+ students, they can't chase you down — but they can issue fee waivers, write recommendations, and answer specific questions if you ask directly and early.
  • Find your own guidance where the system doesn't provide it. A teacher, a local nonprofit, an older student who's been through it, or — the reason this site exists — a free guide written by someone who's done it. You don't need a parent who went to college to get good advice; you need to know where to look.
  • Apply broadly, especially to aid. I applied to about 40 scholarships and won 9. The volume mattered as much as the quality — you can't win the ones you don't apply to.
  • Name your story without being reduced to it. First-gen and low-income students often struggle with how to write about hardship in essays — list too many obstacles in one paragraph, and a reader stops seeing a person and starts seeing a list. Write about what a specific challenge taught you and where it pointed you, not just that it happened.

You're Not Behind — the System Just Didn't Explain Itself

If you're figuring this out without a family roadmap, you're not behind your peers who have one. You're doing the same work they're doing, plus the extra work of finding the map yourself. That's harder, but it's entirely learnable — and it's exactly what the rest of this site is built to help with.
